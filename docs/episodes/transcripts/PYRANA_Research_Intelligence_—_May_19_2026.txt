HOST_A: And we get a number we can put in a procurement deck. Okay — paper two, EHV. This is the governance one.
HOST_B: Yeah, and this is the one I want to be honest about: it's closer to a research architecture sketch than a deployable system, but the core idea is exactly what we need.
HOST_A: Plain version first.
HOST_B: Plain version: today, most AI governance is after-the-fact. The agent does something, you log it, a human reviews it weeks later. EHV says no — put the policy check inside the inference loop itself, so non-compliant actions are literally unable to be generated. Sub-millisecond enforcement, not 14-day audit cycles.
HOST_A: And they claim a formal proof?
HOST_B: They modeled it in TLA+ — that's a formal specification language, used for verifying distributed systems. They ran the model checker over 1,738 states, found zero safety violations, zero deadlocks. The invariant is: for any action, if it's not valid under the policy set, the system status is DENY. Provably.
HOST_A: So how does it actually work in the agent?
HOST_B: Three pillars. First, policies live in what's called a CRDT — a conflict-free replicated data type, which is just a fancy way of saying a data structure that distributed nodes can update without coordinating, because conflicts resolve mathematically. Second, they use TEEs — trusted execution environments, secure enclaves in the CPU — to validate the policy hash. Normally that costs 200+ milliseconds per check, so they cache it per epoch. In their healthcare example, validate once every 60 seconds, then every check inside that window is just an O(1) hash comparison.
HOST_A: And the actual enforcement point?
HOST_B: This is the clever part. It sits at the token-generation layer. When the model is sampling its next token, the policy enforcement point masks the logits — the raw scores — to negative infinity for any token that would produce a non-compliant action. So structurally, not probabilistically, the bad action cannot be sampled.
HOST_A: Wait — that requires you to know which tokens correspond to which actions before they're generated.
HOST_B: Yes, and that's the steel-man. They have a thing called the Action Schema Extraction Layer — ASEL — that parses the model's unstructured output into typed action tuples. And the authors openly admit ASEL is unverified. Their words: a "scoped design boundary." So the entire formal safety guarantee is conditional on ASEL extracting correctly. In adversarial or noisy outputs, that's a big assumption.
HOST_A: Plus the TLA+ model is small — five policy versions, three action types, one agent.
HOST_B: Right, no concurrent multi-agent reasoning. So the proof is real but its scope is narrow.
HOST_A: Builder takeaway for us?
HOST_B: Spike a lightweight governance pass in PlannerDelegatorWorkflow this quarter. PERMIT, DENY, ESCALATE — three outcomes. Don't commit to the TEE stack yet, no secure enclaves, just borrow the epoch-caching idea: validate the CxU policy graph hash once per session, O(1) per call after that. And — this is the part I actually care about — model the invariant in TLA+ using our actual action schema. So when a regulated tenant goes live, we have a formal spec to point at.
HOST_A: Okay, paper three — TRACE. This is the one that surprised me when I read the notes.
HOST_B: Yeah, TRACE is genuinely cool. It's a hallucination-reduction method that is training-free, retrieval-free, and requires zero per-model calibration. It works on any LLM by inspecting the model's own internal forward pass.
HOST_A: Plain English.
HOST_B: When an LLM generates an answer, the answer doesn't appear fully-formed at the last layer. The model's preference for different candidate answers evolves as the input flows through its layers. TRACE watches that evolution — they call it the candidate preference trajectory — and uses the shape of the trajectory to figure out both whether the model is about to hallucinate, and what kind of correction to apply.
HOST_A: How big are the gains?
HOST_B: Mean improvement of +12.26 points on MC1, +8.65 on MC2-style, across 15 models from 8 model families, on three benchmarks. Forty-five total evaluation cells. Zero regressions. That last part is the unusual claim — most of these methods help on average but hurt somewhere.
HOST_A: Mechanism — what does "shape of the trajectory" actually mean?
HOST_B: They compute something called the effective trajectory dimension. If the trajectory is basically one-dimensional — meaning the model is oscillating between two candidates — they apply a scalar correction, either flip the sign or fall back to an earlier layer's state. If the trajectory is multi-dimensional — multiple candidates competing — they apply a candidate-space correction instead. The choice between sign-flip and earlier-state recovery comes from a one-time invariant computed from the model's weights at load time. No labels, no fine-tuning.
HOST_A: Steel-man?
HOST_B: TruthfulQA and similar benchmarks are multiple-choice. They reward correct ranking over a small candidate set. That's structurally easier than open-ended generation, which is what our agents actually do — tool calls, long reasoning chains, code. TRACE requires you to enumerate a candidate set, and in open generation the set isn't predefined. Also, running one forward pass per candidate across every layer adds non-trivial latency for long agentic trajectories.
HOST_A: So the obvious application for us is the bounded case — where we do have a candidate set.
HOST_B: Exactly. When the RetrievalAgent surfaces two conflicting CxUs about, say, a drug dosage, that's a bounded candidate set. TRACE's candidate-space correction is directly applicable. Prototype it as optional PyranaHarness middleware for high-stakes tool-call arguments — drug names, dosages, regulatory citations — top-k CxU-grounded alternatives only, so cost stays bounded.

HOST_B: Yes, and this one's the empirical heavyweight of the batch. 3,000 trials, 10 agents, five vendors, on a real coupled hydrology simulation pipeline.

HOST_B: They built a knowledge scaffold — they call it Knowledge Infrastructure, KI — that wraps around process-based scientific models.
