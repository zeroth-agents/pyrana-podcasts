HOST_A: Okay. Paper two — MMPO, the memory paper.
HOST_B: This one I'm excited about. Plain-English: when an AI agent runs a long task — like thousands of turns, millions of tokens — it can't keep everything in its head. It has to keep summarizing what happened so far. And the problem is, those summaries quietly get worse over time, and the agent doesn't notice until it fails the task. HOST_A: How? Like, how do you train "notice your summary got worse"?
HOST_B: This is the clever bit. After every summary the agent writes, they ask it a fixed probe question — paraphrasing, but basically: "what is the current task progress, and what information do you still need?" Then they measure how confident the agent is in its own answer. If it's confident, the summary preserved the important stuff. If it's uncertain — high entropy in its response — the summary lost something.
HOST_A: Wait, back up. Entropy here means what, exactly?
HOST_B: Good question. Entropy is just a measure of uncertainty. When the model predicts the next word, it assigns probabilities to all possible words. If it's confident, one word gets most of the probability — low entropy. If it's spread across many possibilities, that's high entropy — it doesn't know what to say. So they're using the model's own uncertainty about its progress as the signal.
HOST_A: And that uncertainty becomes a training reward.
HOST_B: Becomes a dense per-turn reward, yeah. Combined with the final outcome signal via group-relative policy optimization — that's a reinforcement learning method that compares multiple attempts at the same task. The headline number: 97.1% task performance retained at 1.75 million token contexts, where baseline methods degrade. And the entropy reduction correlates with final accuracy at Pearson r of negative 0.684, which for this kind of signal is genuinely strong.
