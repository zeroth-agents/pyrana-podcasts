HOST_A: Okay. So the plan is — walk through each one, plain English first, then mechanism, then what we actually do about it. Let's start with Helicase.
HOST_B: Helicase is from Cambridge and the Alan Turing Institute. The plain version: they built a multi-agent system that, given a question about a supply chain, decomposes it, sends specialist agents to investigate, and outputs a knowledge graph where every fact has a confidence score attached. Not a prose answer — a graph with calibrated uncertainty per edge.
HOST_A: And the headline result?
HOST_B: On their benchmark, called SCQA — 80 queries across four difficulty quadrants — Helicase is the only system that completes end-to-end discovery across all four. Frontier models like ChatGPT and Gemini score near zero on the hardest quadrant, which is multi-hop questions over low-visibility data.
HOST_A: Multi-hop meaning the answer requires chaining several facts together?
HOST_B: Exactly. Like, "who supplies the supplier of the lithium for this battery" — three hops deep, and the data isn't on the front page of anyone's website.
HOST_A: Okay so how does Helicase actually pull that off? Walk me through the architecture.
HOST_B: Three layers. A planner agent takes the query and writes an ordered investigation plan as sub-questions. Specialist agents — one for web search, one for reasoning, and importantly a coding agent — execute those sub-questions. The coding agent isn't just retrieving text; it's actively building intermediate knowledge graph pieces.
HOST_A: And the uncertainty part?
HOST_B: This is the bit I want us to steal. They track uncertainty at three layers. Action-layer is "how reliable was this individual retrieval, this source." Trajectory-layer is "across the whole pipeline, how consistent were the agents about this claim." And memory-layer is "as new evidence comes in, does confidence in this stored fact go up or down."
HOST_A: Wait, back up — that third one. The memory layer. That's saying the confidence score isn't static, it updates as the graph grows?
HOST_B: Yes. Think of it like a detective board where every pinned note has a confidence number on it, and when a new clue comes in, the numbers on related notes get re-weighted automatically. That's the memory layer.
HOST_A: That maps almost one-to-one onto our LoopAgentState, AgentContext, and TEMPR layers, right?
HOST_B: It does, which is the validating part. Now — steel-man the objection. SCQA is an 80-query benchmark built by the same lab. There's no independent replication. And the "calibrated uncertainty" claim — they show the numbers exist and they correlate with source quality qualitatively, but they don't show a real calibration curve. No Expected Calibration Error metric.
HOST_A: So the architecture is convincing but the calibration evidence is thinner than the framing suggests.
HOST_B: Right. And there's prior work — Zheng and Brintrup did a vaccine-supplier KG with LLM agents already. So the genuine novel piece is the three-layer uncertainty propagation, not the agent decomposition.
HOST_A: Builder takeaway for us?
HOST_B: This quarter: prototype a per-fact q-value field on the CONTEXT_FOR edges in Cortex, modeled on Helicase's three layers. And — this is the cheap one — adopt their complexity quadrant framing as a test harness for CxU extraction. Single-hop versus multi-hop, high-visibility versus low-visibility data. Four quadrants, measure how our retrieval quality degrades as hop depth increases.
HOST_A: Good. Next up — Chat-ISV. The steel industry one.
HOST_B: Yeah. So plain version: a Chinese team built a chatbot for industrial volatile organic compounds — basically air pollution in steel manufacturing — backed by a knowledge graph built from 382 papers. The interesting result isn't the chatbot itself, it's a graph engineering fix that took their isolated-node rate from 57% down to about 4%.
HOST_A: Isolated nodes meaning entities in the graph with no connections?
HOST_B: Right. Floating facts. You've extracted "benzene" as an entity but it has no edges to anything, so it's useless for retrieval. 57% of their nodes started that way, which is honestly catastrophic but probably realistic for naive LLM extraction.
HOST_A: How'd they fix it?
HOST_B: Two moves. First, at extraction time, they inject semantic co-occurrence rules into the prompt so the model infers implicit relations even when the source sentence doesn't have an explicit connector word. Second, and this is the structural one — when they import a chunk of text into Neo4j, they create an intermediate node for the chunk itself, and connect it via "mentions" edges to every entity that appears in it.
HOST_A: So the chunk becomes a hub, and entities that would've been isolated are now at least connected through their shared source paragraph.
HOST_B: Exactly. It's a star topology centered on the source chunk. You lose some relation specificity — you don't know exactly how benzene and the furnace are related, just that they're discussed together — but you gain global connectivity, and crucially, every entity becomes traceable to its evidence span in the original text.
HOST_A: Which is the audit trail piece.
HOST_B: That's the part that matters for CxU governance. Their precision was 96.93%, recall 72.63%, F1 of 0.83, on 400 expert blind evaluations.
HOST_A: That recall is kind of low though.
HOST_B: It is, and here's the steel-man. Their scoring rubric is non-standard. Score of 2 maps to true positive, 1 to false negative, 0 to false positive. That mapping inflates precision because any partially correct answer gets counted as a miss, not a wrong answer. So you can't really compare their 96.93% to anyone else's precision number directly.
HOST_A: Hm. And the recall gap — is that a retrieval failure or an extraction failure?
HOST_B: They don't fully diagnose it. Could be either. And the corpus is narrow — 382 papers in one industrial domain. Whether the isolated-node fix generalizes to something as heterogeneous as the Cortex corpus is an open question.
HOST_A: But the metric itself — isolated node ratio — that's something we could measure today.
HOST_B: That's exactly the takeaway. Add isolated-node-ratio and lineage-coverage as first-class quality signals in the Cortex extraction pipeline this quarter. And audit our schema — do we have a chunk-level intermediate node that can carry "mentions" edges back to source spans? If not, that's the structural change with the clearest measured payoff in today's digest.
HOST_A: Okay, third paper. This is the harness sensitivity one, and I think this is the one that's actually going to ship code fastest.
HOST_B: Probably, yeah. Plain version: someone ran a controlled experiment — 432 runs, 6 models, 3 different prompt strictness levels — and found that more structured prompts make some good models worse and other good models better. The conventional wisdom that "frontier models need less hand-holding" is just empirically wrong, depending on the model type.
HOST_A: Define harness for me. Prompt strictness — like, how detailed the system prompt is?
HOST_B: Right. A harness is the system prompt wrapper that tells the agent what its job is, what format to output in, what tools it can use, how to verify its work. Light harness is two lines: here's your role, here's your task. Strict harness is six stages: preflight, plan, execute, verify, recover, report, with explicit success criteria.
HOST_A: And the finding?
HOST_B: Gemini 2.5 Flash — a frontier chat model — drops from 95.8% success on light harness to 58% on balanced and 66% on strict. On some task categories it goes from 100% to literally zero.
HOST_A: Zero?
HOST_B: Zero on inspect-local tasks under strict harness. Meanwhile Qwen3.5-122B with extended thinking goes the opposite direction — peaks at 91.7% under strict harness, and gets faster, not slower. Latency drops from 38.5 seconds to 23.3 seconds.
HOST_A: That's wild. Why does strict make the chat model worse?
HOST_B: The hypothesis is that multi-stage process instructions shift the chat model's output distribution toward explanatory prose. It starts narrating its plan instead of just emitting the required JSON. The dominant failure mode is "format_violation" — the model is doing the task correctly but wrapping it in commentary that breaks the parser.
HOST_A: And for reasoning models, the strict harness is — what, scaffolding?
HOST_B: Exactly. The explicit success criteria act like chain-of-thought support. The model knows when it's done, so it stops thinking sooner. That's why latency drops.
HOST_A: Okay, steel-man this one.
HOST_B: One model per tier. The authors flag it themselves — you can't really make tier-level claims from n equals one. k equals one repeat, so confidence intervals are wide. Some of these "non-monotone" cells aren't statistically distinguishable from noise. And HEAT-24, their benchmark, is synthetic — 24 deterministic git tasks. The format_violation finding could just be an artifact of how JSON-heavy their benchmark is.
HOST_A: Fair. But for us?
HOST_B: For us it's still the strongest signal in today's digest. MessageBuilder currently applies one harness strictness regardless of which model is in the slot. This sprint — not this quarter, this sprint — make harness strictness a per-model-tier and per-model-type config, splitting chat versus reasoning. And add a format_violation auto-repair step in the tool-call executor. If a model emits prose around valid JSON, strip the prose and retry. Lowest-effort change with the clearest justification.
HOST_A: Last one. ORCA, from Bosch.
HOST_B: Right. Plain version: ORCA is a copilot for root cause analysis in manufacturing. You point it at a problem, it figures out the causal structure, estimates effects, identifies root causes, writes a report. The interesting design choice is that you can run it anywhere on a spectrum from fully automatic to fully user-guided, step by step.
HOST_A: And it's a real production system at Bosch?
HOST_B: It's a workshop paper at Causal NeSy 2026, so it's polished but it's not benchmarked against competitors. No head-to-head numbers. That's the main steel-man — there's no quantitative comparison to existing causal tooling like DoWhy or CausalNex.
HOST_A: Walk me through what's architecturally interesting.
HOST_B: Three layers. A request manager that routes LLM calls and offloads heavy causal algorithms to GPU clusters. A workflow manager that holds session state and logs every step with its results — so you can navigate backward without losing global context. And individual agents that wrap specific causal methods from a curated library.
HOST_A: And the part that's relevant to us?
HOST_B: Two things. First, the human-in-the-loop dial. PYRANA today treats HITL as binary — either a step needs approval or it doesn't. HOST_A: So you'd want, like, three named levels — auto, copilot, gated — and make them per-stage.
HOST_B: Exactly. The second thing is the computation time estimation module. Before committing to a causal algorithm, ORCA estimates how long it'll take on the user's hardware and surfaces the accuracy-versus-latency tradeoff as an explicit decision. That's a UX pattern we should consider for any expensive Cortex operation.
